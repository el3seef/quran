Back from Al-Hudaibiyah, at peace with Quraish, he decided to write to the kings beyond Arabia and call them to Islam. To vouch for his envoys, a silver seal was made, engraved with three words, one above another: Allah, Messenger, Muhammad. He chose envoys for their knowledge and experience, and in Muharram of the seventh year, a few days before he marched on Khaibar, they set out.

## negus-letter
title: The Negus Answers
word: Negus
mood: oasis
when: 7 AH
y: 628.421
place: Abyssinia
look: quote
tags: letters, kingdoms
'Amr bin Umaiyah carried the letter to Ashamah, the Negus. It greeted him with peace for whoever follows guidance, bore witness that Allah has no partner, no wife and no son, and that Muhammad is His servant and Messenger, called him to Islam, and ended with the ayah calling the People of the Book to a word just between them [3:64].

The Negus took the letter and laid it on his eyes. He came down from his throne to the ground, declared his Islam, and wrote back: by the Lord of heaven and earth, 'Isa is no more than what you say of him; he had taken in Ja'far and his companions, and he bore witness that Muhammad was the Messenger of Allah, and gave his pledge to Allah through Ja'far.

At the Prophet's request he sent Ja'far and the emigrants home; they reached him at Khaibar. The Negus died in Rajab of the ninth year, and the Prophet ﷺ told the Muslims of his death and prayed the funeral prayer for him from afar.
quote: By the Lord of the heavens and the earth, 'Isa is no more than what you say of him. — The Negus, in his reply

## muqawqis
title: The Ruler of Egypt
word: Egypt
mood: sand
when: 7 AH
y: 628.422
place: Alexandria
look: quote
tags: letters, kingdoms, family
Hatib bin Abi Balta'ah took the letter to Al-Muqawqis, ruler of Egypt and leader of the Copts. Before he handed it over he spoke to him: there had been one before you who called himself the supreme lord, and Allah made an example of him; take warning from others, not let others take warning from you. We will not leave our religion, said the Muqawqis, except for a better one. Hatib answered that Islam was enough in place of everything else; that as Musa foretold 'Isa, 'Isa foretold Muhammad; and that they were calling him to Islam as he called the people of the Torah to the Gospel.

The Muqawqis thought on it. This prophet, he said, called to nothing hateful, nor was he a wandering sorcerer or a lying soothsayer; he had the marks of prophethood. He had the letter put in an ivory casket and wrote back that he knew a prophet was still to come, but had thought he would rise in Syria. He sent gifts: two maids of noble Coptic families, clothing, and a mount to ride, named Duldul, which lived on into the days of Mu'awiyah.

He did not accept Islam. One of the maids, Mariyah, stayed with the Prophet ﷺ and bore him his son Ibrahim; the other, Sirin, he gave to Hassan bin Thabit.
quote: This prophet calls to nothing hateful; he is no wandering sorcerer and no lying soothsayer, and he has the marks of prophethood. — Al-Muqawqis

'Abdullah bin Hudhafah As-Sahmi took the letter towards Chosroes by way of the governor of Bahrain. The king of Persia was enraged that the Prophet's name was written above his own. He tore the letter to pieces, and ordered his governor in Yemen, Badhan, to send two men to bring this Arab to him.

## heraclius
title: Heraclius Questions Abu Sufyan
word: Heraclius
mood: parchment
when: 7 AH, during the truce
y: 628.424
place: Iliya' (Jerusalem)
look: quote
tags: letters, kingdoms, makkah
Dihyah Al-Kalbi carried the letter to the governor of Busra, to send on to Heraclius. It happened that Abu Sufyan was trading in Syria with some men of Quraish, and Heraclius, at Iliya', sent for them. Who was nearest of kin to this man who called himself a prophet? Abu Sufyan was, so he was seated in front, his companions behind him, told to call out if he lied. "By Allah," Abu Sufyan said later, "had I not feared they would call me a liar, I would have lied about him."

So he told the truth: of noble lineage; no king among his forefathers; followed by the weak, more of them every day; no one had ever caught him in a lie or a betrayal; the war between them went by turns; he told people to worship Allah alone and leave what their fathers worshipped, and to pray, to be truthful, chaste and keep the ties of kinship. Heraclius weighed each answer: Prophets come from noble lines; a man who will not lie about people would not lie about Allah; the weak follow prophets; faith, once its sweetness enters the heart, is not abandoned. If this was true, the man would rule the ground beneath his feet. He had known a prophet was coming, but never thought he would be an Arab. If he could, he would go to him and wash his feet.

Then the letter was read, and the court was in uproar, and the Arabs were put out. "Ibn Abi Kabshah's affair has grown so great," Abu Sufyan said to his friends, "that the king of the Romans fears him." From then on he was sure Muhammad would win, until Allah brought him to Islam. Heraclius did not believe. On his way home Dihyah was robbed by men of Judham at Hisma; Zaid bin Harithah went against them with five hundred men, and when a chief of Judham who was a Muslim complained, the Prophet ﷺ had everything taken given back.
quote: If what you say is true, he will rule the very ground beneath these feet of mine. If I could reach him, I would wash his feet. — Heraclius, to Abu Sufyan

The answers were as different as the men. Some believed; some held on to what they had. But all of them now had to think about Islam, and a new prophet.

In Oman, 'Amr bin Al-'As went first to 'Abd, the gentler of the brothers. What had his father thought of this faith? He had died before it, said 'Amr; he wished he had believed, and he himself had been the same until Allah guided him, at the court of the Negus, who had believed too, and his bishops and monks with him. "Beware of lying, 'Amr; nothing shames a man more." "I do not lie, and our religion forbids it." What did his Prophet command? To obey Allah, keep faith with kin, and leave wrongdoing, adultery, wine and the worship of idols and the cross. "These are fair things he calls to." His brother Jaifar was harder, and kept 'Amr waiting days; but after the two brothers talked alone, both accepted Islam.

## dhu-qarad
title: The Son of Al-Akwa'

y: 628.45
place: Dhu Qarad, a day's ride from Madinah

At dawn 'Abdur-Rahman Al-Fazari raided the Prophet's camels at their pasture, killed the man watching them and drove them off. Salamah bin Al-Akwa', who was there with Talhah's horse, sent the Prophet's servant Rabah galloping to Madinah, climbed a hill, shouted three times towards the city for help, and set off after the raiders alone on foot.

He shot at them as he ran, chanting, "I am the son of Al-Akwa'; today the mean will meet their end." When a horseman turned on him he hid at the foot of a tree and shot his horse. When they went into a narrow pass he climbed above them and rolled down stones. He chased them until every camel was free, and they threw away more than thirty cloaks and thirty spears to lighten themselves; he marked each with a stone for the Muslims to find. Then the Prophet's horsemen came through the trees. Akhram fought 'Abdur-Rahman and was killed by him; Abu Qatadah killed 'Abdur-Rahman. Salamah drove the rest from the water of Dhu Qarad before they could drink.

He begged for a hundred men to finish them. "Ibn Al-Akwa', you have had your fill; now be gentle. They are already feasting among Ghatafan." He gave Salamah two shares, a rider's and a footman's, and had him ride home behind him on his camel Al-'Adba'.
quote: Our best horseman today is Abu Qatadah, and our best footman Salamah. — The Prophet ﷺ

## to-khaibar
title: The Road to Khaibar
word: Khaibar
mood: dusk
when: End of Muharram 7 AH · 628 CE
y: 628.47
place: The road north to Khaibar
look: map khaibar
tags: battles, signs
Quraish were at peace with him now. The Jews of Khaibar, sixty to eighty miles north of Madinah, were not: they had stirred Quraizah to betray him, stayed in league with Ghatafan and plotted against his life. He took with him only those who had pledged at Al-Hudaibiyah, about fourteen hundred men, and Allah had promised them gains [48:20]; those who had hung back before now asked to come, and were refused [48:15]. Siba' bin 'Arfatah stayed over Madinah. It was then that Abu Hurairah came and accepted Islam.

'Abdullah bin Ubai sent word to Khaibar: you outnumber them, and are better armed. They sent to Ghatafan, promising half Khaibar's dates for their help. So he camped at Ar-Raji', between Ghatafan and Khaibar, to cut them off.

They marched by night. 'Amir bin Al-Akwa' sang to urge the camels on: O Allah, without You we would not have been guided, nor given charity, nor prayed. "Who is this driver?" "'Amir." "May Allah have mercy on him." The men knew what that meant when he said it of a Companion on the march. At As-Sahba' he called for their food, and what little they brought increased in his hands until all had eaten. Then, as Khaibar came in sight below, he stopped and prayed for the good of this town and its people, and refuge from its evil.

At dawn the people of Khaibar came out with their spades and baskets to their fields, saw the army and ran back shouting, "Muhammad and his army!"
quote: Allahu akbar, Khaibar is ruined. When we come down into a people's land, evil is the morning of those who were warned. — The Prophet ﷺ, at dawn below Khaibar

## banner
title: "A Man Who Loves Allah and His Messenger"
word: 'Ali
mood: iron

y: 628.5

Khaibar was two districts of forts. In the first, An-Natah and Ash-Shiqq, stood Na'im, As-Sa'b bin Mu'adh, Az-Zubair, Ubai and An-Nizar; in the second, Al-Katibah, stood Al-Qamus, the fort of the family of Abul-Huqaiq, Al-Watih and As-Sulalim. Al-Hubab bin Al-Mundhir advised moving the camp, and he moved it.

"Tomorrow I will give the banner to a man who loves Allah and His Messenger, and whom Allah and His Messenger love." All night they wondered who it would be. In the morning he called for 'Ali, whose eyes were sore; he prayed for him, and they were healed. 'Ali said he would fight them until they became Muslims. "Go gently until you reach them; call them to Islam and tell them their duty to Allah. By Allah, that Allah should guide one man through you is better for you than the finest camels."

At Na'im, Marhab the Jew called for combat. 'Amir bin Al-Akwa' answered; his own sword rebounded and cut his knee, and he died of it. "He has a double reward," the Prophet ﷺ said, holding two fingers together. 'Ali killed Marhab, and Az-Zubair killed his brother Yasir. Fort by fort they fell: As-Sa'b to Al-Hubab after three days; Az-Zubair after a Jew showed them the spring beneath it and it was cut; Ubai to Abu Dujanah; An-Nizar, high and strong, to the catapults. Hungry, some men cooked the flesh of tame donkeys; he had the pots overturned and forbade it. Then he besieged Al-Katibah fourteen days, and as the catapults were readied they asked for terms.
quote: That Allah should guide one man through you is better for you than the finest camels. — The Prophet ﷺ, giving 'Ali the banner at Khaibar

Ibn Abul-Huqaiq came to make terms: their lives would be spared if they left Khaibar and its lands and left their gold and silver behind, and the treaty would hold only if they hid nothing. But the sons of Abul-Huqaiq hid a leather bag of jewels and money that Huyai bin Akhtab had carried out of Madinah. ## riqa
title: The Expedition of Rags

With Quraish at peace and the Jews subdued, there remained the Bedouin of Najd, scattered in the desert, raiding and running. Word came that Banu Muharib and Banu Tha'labah of Ghatafan were gathering. He set out with four to seven hundred men, leaving Abu Dharr, or 'Uthman, over Madinah, and went deep into their land to Nakhlah. They met the Bedouin there but no one fought, and he led the prayer of fear. Abu Musa Al-Ash'ari remembered six of them sharing one camel by turns, their feet so torn that their nails came off and they bound them in rags.

Jabir told how they rested at noon under the trees, and the Prophet ﷺ hung his sword on a branch and slept. He woke to find a Bedouin standing over him with the sword drawn. "Who will protect you from me?" "Allah." The sword fell from the man's hand, and the Prophet ﷺ took it up: "And who will protect you from me?" "You are the better one to hold a sword." He would not fight him again, he said, nor join anyone against him, and was let go, and told his people he had come from the best of men.

A captive woman's husband swore to have a Muslim's blood. By night he found 'Abbad bin Bishr standing guard in prayer and shot him. 'Abbad pulled the arrow out and prayed on, and again, and again, three arrows, before he finished and woke his companion 'Ammar. Why had he not woken him at the first? He was in the middle of a surah, he said, and did not want to cut it short.
quote: Allah. — The Prophet ﷺ, when the Bedouin asked who would protect him

## qada

A year after Al-Hudaibiyah he went to make the 'Umrah the treaty allowed, with all who had been at Al-Hudaibiyah: two thousand men, some women and children, and sixty camels for sacrifice. 'Uwaif bin Al-Adbat, or Abu Ruhm, stayed over Madinah. Fearing a trick, they took their weapons, but left them with two hundred men eight miles out, and went in with sheathed swords, he riding Al-Qaswa', the Companions all round him calling, "Here I am, O Allah, at Your service!"

Quraish had left the town for the hills and watched from Mount Qu'aiqi'an. They had said the fever of Yathrib had worn the Muslims out, so he told them to run the first three rounds of the tawaf and walk the rest, to show their strength. 'Abdullah bin Rawahah walked before him chanting verses. They went between As-Safa and Al-Marwah, sacrificed at Al-Marwah and shaved, and then those who had kept the weapons came in and did the same.

On the fourth morning the chiefs of Quraish sent 'Ali to tell him his three days were over. He kept the treaty and left, and stopped at Sarif. On this visit his uncle Al-'Abbas offered him Maimunah bint Al-Harith, his wife's sister, in marriage, and the wedding was at Sarif. It is called the 'Umrah of the treaty, or the 'Umrah made good. After it a few more columns went out: fifty under Ibn Abul-'Awja' to Banu Sulaim, two hundred under Ghalib to Fadak, fifteen under Ka'b bin 'Umair against Quda'ah, all killed but one, and twenty-five under Shuja' bin Wahb against Hawazin.

## mutah
title: Mu'tah
word: Mu'tah
mood: iron
when: Jumada al-Ula 8 AH · September 629 CE
y: 629.7
place: Mu'tah, in Al-Balqa'
look: map mutah
tags: battles, kingdoms
He had sent Al-Harith bin 'Umair Al-Azdi with a letter to the ruler of Busra. On the way Shurahbil bin 'Amr Al-Ghassani, governor of Al-Balqa' for Caesar, had him bound and beheaded. To kill an envoy was to declare war. He raised three thousand men, the largest army the Muslims had yet sent out but for the Trench, and gave a white banner to Zaid bin Harithah: if Zaid fell, Ja'far bin Abi Talib; if Ja'far fell, 'Abdullah bin Rawahah. At the place of Al-Harith's murder they were first to call the people to Islam.

Madinah came out to see them off. 'Abdullah bin Rawahah wept: not for love of this world, he said, but for the ayah he had heard the Prophet ﷺ recite, that every one of us will come to the Fire [19:71]. He told them to fight in Allah's name; not to steal from the spoils; not to kill women, children, old men or hermits; and not to cut down trees or pull down houses. The Prophet ﷺ walked with them as far as Ath-Thaniyah, and prayed for them.

At Ma'an, on the edge of Syria, they learned that Heraclius had come with a hundred thousand men, and a hundred thousand more of the Arab tribes who served Rome. They had never imagined such an army. For two nights they talked of writing to the Prophet ﷺ for help or orders. Then 'Abdullah bin Rawahah stood up.
quote: By Allah, what you are shrinking from is the very thing you came out to seek: martyrdom. We do not fight by number or strength, but by this religion Allah has honoured us with. Go on: it is one of two good things, victory or martyrdom. — 'Abdullah bin Rawahah, at Ma'an

## commanders
title: Zaid, Ja'far and 'Abdullah
word: Two Wings
mood: grief
when: Jumada al-Ula 8 AH
y: 629.71
place: Mu'tah
look: quote
tags: battles, family, companions
They met the enemy near Masharif and drew back to Mu'tah to fight: three thousand against fifty times as many. Zaid bin Harithah, dearest of men to the Prophet's heart, led them in and fought until the spears brought him down. Ja'far took the banner. He jumped down from his horse and killed it, and fought until his right hand was cut off; he held the banner in his left, until that was cut off too; then he held it in his arms, until a Roman struck him and cut him in two. There were fifty wounds on him, and not one in his back. Allah gave him two wings in Paradise in place of his hands, to fly where he wishes there, and he is called Ja'far of the Two Wings.

'Abdullah bin Rawahah took the banner, urged himself on in verse, and fought until he was killed. Thabit bin Al-Arqam lifted it and called on the Muslims to choose a leader, and they chose Khalid bin Al-Walid. Nine swords broke in Khalid's hand that day. He changed his ranks, right to left and rear to front, so that the Romans thought fresh men had come; and he drew the army back slowly, skirmishing, until the Romans, fearing a trap in the desert, stopped following. Only twelve Muslims were killed.

Al-Harith was not avenged, but no one had thought three thousand men could stand against two hundred thousand of the greatest power on earth, and come home. Banu Sulaim, Ashja', Ghatafan, Dhubyan, Fazarah and other tribes accepted Islam after it.
quote: He fought until his right hand was cut off, then his left; then he held the banner in his arms, until he was killed. — of Ja'far bin Abi Talib, at Mu'tah

The Arab tribes on the Syrian border had stood with Rome; now Quda'ah were gathering. He sent 'Amr bin Al-'As, whose grandmother was of Bali, one of the tribes there, with three hundred Emigrants and Helpers and thirty horse, under a white flag and a black banner, to win over Bali, 'Udhrah and Balqain and strike at Quda'ah.
