## arabia-land
title: The Land of the Arabs
word: Arabia
mood: sand
when: Before Islam
place: The Arabian Peninsula
look: map arabia
tags: places
The word "Arab" first meant desert: open, waterless land without plants. Since ancient times it has named both the peninsula and the people who live in it.

The peninsula is bounded by the Red Sea and Sinai to the west, the Arabian Gulf to the east, the Arabian Sea to the south, and Syria and part of Iraq to the north. Its area is reckoned at between one and one-and-a-third million square miles.

Its deserts kept invaders out, so its people stayed free for ages beside two great empires. Yet it sat at the meeting point of the old world, joined by land and sea to most nations of the time. It became a crossroads of trade, culture and religion.
facts:
- West: The Red Sea and Sinai
- East: The Arabian Gulf
- South: The Arabian Sea
- North: Syria and part of Iraq

## arabia-peoples
title: Three Peoples
word: Qahtan
mood: sand
when: Before Islam
place: Arabia
look: map tribes
tags: people
Arab genealogists divide the Arabs by descent into three groups.

The Perished Arabs are the ancient peoples whose history is mostly lost, such as 'Ad and Thamud. The Pure Arabs, or Qahtanis, descend from Ya'rub bin Yashjub bin Qahtan and first lived in Yemen. The Arabized Arabs, or 'Adnanis, descend from Isma'il (Ishmael), son of Ibrahim (Abraham).

Of the Qahtani tribes of Yemen, Kahlan moved north as trade failed under Roman control of the land and sea routes. Al-Azd scattered widely. Their sons Aws and Khazraj settled in Yathrib, the later Madinah. Khuza'ah took Makkah from the tribe of Jurhum. Others founded the Ghassanid kingdom in Syria. Lakhm founded the kingdom of Heerah in Iraq, while Tai' settled in the mountains of the north.
list:
- The Perished Arabs: 'Ad, Thamud, Tasm, Jadis and others, little known
- The Pure Arabs: Qahtan's sons, first of Yemen: Himyar and Kahlan
- The Arabized Arabs: Isma'il's sons, the 'Adnanis

## arabia-ibrahim
title: Ibrahim, Hajar and Isma'il
word: Zamzam
mood: dusk
img: mecca-1888
when: About 1800 BCE
y: -1800
place: The valley of Makkah
look: image valley
tags: prophets, makkah
Ibrahim (Abraham) came from Ur, near Kufah in Iraq, then went to Harran and Palestine. On a journey to Egypt, Pharaoh plotted against his wife Sarah, but Allah protected her. Pharaoh then gave her Hajar (Hagar) as a servant. Sarah gave Hajar to Ibrahim in marriage, and Hajar bore him Isma'il.

By Allah's command Ibrahim took Hajar and the infant to a barren valley beside the Sacred House, where there were no people and no water. He left them with a bag of dates and a skin of water. When these ran out, water burst from the ground: the spring of Zamzam. The tribe of Jurhum came to live beside them.

Ibrahim visited them from Palestine several times. In a dream he was shown himself sacrificing his son. Both submitted to Allah, and Allah ransomed Isma'il with a great sacrifice [37:103-107]. On a later visit, father and son raised the walls of the Ka'bah, and Ibrahim called people to make pilgrimage to it.

## arabia-quraish
title: From Isma'il to Quraish
word: Quraish
mood: parchment
when: Before Islam
place: Makkah
look: lineage
tags: people, makkah
Isma'il married into Jurhum and had twelve sons. Over time only the lines of two of them, Nabet and Qidar, could still be traced. The Nabataeans, sons of Nabet, built a kingdom in the north of Hijaz with Petra as its capital, until Rome overthrew it.

From Qidar came 'Adnan, the twenty-first grandfather of the Prophet ﷺ. When the Prophet ﷺ traced his own lineage, it is reported that he stopped at 'Adnan. 'Adnan's line branched into many tribes. From Mudar came Kinanah, and from Kinanah came Quraish, the sons of Fihr bin Malik. Quraish divided into clans, among them Makhzum, Jumah, Sahm, 'Adi, Taim, Zuhrah and the sons of Qusai. From Qusai's son 'Abd Manaf came Hashim, and from Hashim, the Prophet ﷺ.
quote: Allah chose Kinanah from the sons of Isma'il, Quraish from Kinanah, Hashim from Quraish, and He chose me from Hashim. — The Prophet ﷺ, reported by Muslim
lineage: Ibrahim > Isma'il > Qidar > … > 'Adnan > Ma'ad > Nizar > Mudar > … > Kinanah > … > Fihr, called Quraish > … > Qusai > 'Abd Manaf > Hashim

## arabia-yemen
title: The Kings of Yemen
word: Sheba
mood: dusk
when: 1300 BCE – 638 CE
y: 450
place: Yemen
look: map kingdoms
tags: kingdoms
Sheba was among the oldest of the peoples of Yemen. Its kings built the great dam of Ma'rib, ruled from Sarwah and then from Ma'rib, and set up colonies across the region. Himyar later took the kingdom. Trade declined as the Nabataeans and then Rome took the trade routes, and the tribes fought among themselves.

The dam broke in the Great Flood around 450 CE, which the Qur'an mentions. Yemen's people scattered. In 523 CE the Jewish king Dhu Nawas threw the Christians of Najran into a pit of fire when they would not leave their faith. The Qur'an speaks of the "people of the ditch" [85:4]. Abyssinia (Ethiopia), with Roman support, conquered Yemen in 525 CE. One of its commanders, Abrahah, became its ruler. It was he who later marched on the Ka'bah.

Around 575 CE the Yemenis rose with Persian help under Ma'dikarib bin Saif Dhu Yazin. After he was killed, Persia ruled Yemen through its governors, until the last of them, Badhan, embraced Islam.
facts:
- The dam bursts: about 450 CE
- The ditch of Najran: 523 CE
- Abyssinian rule: from 525 CE
- Persian rule: from about 575 CE

## arabia-borders
title: Heerah and the Ghassanids
word: Heerah
mood: dusk
when: 3rd – 7th century CE
y: 500
place: Iraq and Syria
look: map kingdoms
tags: kingdoms
On the Persian side, the Sassanid kings ruled the Arabs of Iraq through Arab kings of their own choosing at Heerah, the Lakhmids. They were a shield against raids from the desert and against the Byzantines' Arabs. When the Persian king later imprisoned the last of them, An-Nu'man, until he died, the tribe of Shaiban refused to hand over what he had left in its keeping. At Dhi Qar the Arabs routed a Persian army for the first time. This is said to have been shortly after the Prophet's ﷺ birth.

On the Roman side, Arabs of Quda'ah first guarded the Byzantine frontier. The Ghassanids then took their place and ruled the Arabs of Syria for the empire. They lasted until the battle of Yarmuk in 13 AH. Their last king, Jabalah bin Al-Aihum, embraced Islam in the time of 'Umar.

The kings around Arabia were never truly free: they ruled their people for empires beyond them. Deep in the desert, the tribes governed themselves.

## arabia-makkah-rule
title: Who Ruled Makkah
word: Qusai
mood: sand
img: kaaba-1910
when: Before 440 CE
y: 440
place: Makkah
look: text
tags: makkah
After Isma'il, his sons kept the House for a time, then rule passed to their mother's people, Jurhum. When Jurhum grew harsh to visitors and took the Ka'bah's wealth, the 'Adnanis and Khuza'ah drove them out. Before leaving, Jurhum filled in the well of Zamzam and buried things in it.

Khuza'ah then ruled Makkah for about three centuries. Then Qusai bin Kilab of Quraish married the daughter of Khuza'ah's chief, Hulail. After Hulail's death, Qusai took Makkah and the House, with Quraish and Kinanah behind him. The wars ended in arbitration, which gave him both. From about 440 CE, Quraish held Makkah and the Ka'bah without rival.

Qusai brought his kin into Makkah and built Dar an-Nadwah, the House of Assembly, beside the Ka'bah. It was where Quraish met, took counsel and made marriages.

## arabia-qusai
title: The Honours of Qusai
word: Nadwah
mood: parchment
when: About 440 CE
y: 441
place: Makkah
look: list
tags: makkah
Qusai held every honour of Makkah, and his word was obeyed in all of them. He passed them to his son 'Abd Manaf rather than his eldest, 'Abdud-Dar. After 'Abd Manaf, the two branches nearly came to war, and a settlement divided the honours between them.

Feeding and watering the pilgrims went to the sons of 'Abd Manaf: first Hashim, then his brother Al-Muttalib, then Hashim's son 'Abdul-Muttalib, the Prophet's ﷺ grandfather. When Islam came, it was in the hands of Al-'Abbas. The assembly, the standard and the keys of the Ka'bah stayed with the sons of 'Abdud-Dar. Other offices went to other clans, such as the arbitration of blood money, the cavalry, and embassies to other tribes.
list:
- Dar an-Nadwah: presiding over the assembly, where counsel was taken and marriages announced
- The Standard: no war was declared without him or his sons
- The Caravans: no caravan left Makkah except under his command
- The Keys: he alone could open the Ka'bah and keep it
- Water (Siqayah): basins of water, sweetened with dates or raisins, for the pilgrims
- Food (Rifadah): food for pilgrims who had none, from a tax Quraish paid each season

## arabia-idols
title: How the Idols Came
word: Hubal
mood: ember
when: Before Islam
y: 300
place: Makkah
look: map idols
tags: faith
Most Arabs had followed the religion of Ibrahim: they worshipped Allah alone. Over long ages they forgot parts of it, though its core remained. Then 'Amr bin Luhai, a chief of Khuza'ah much loved for his piety and generosity, saw people worshipping idols in Syria. He brought back an idol, Hubal, set it up inside the Ka'bah and called people to worship it.

Idolatry spread through Makkah and then all of Hijaz. Manat stood at Al-Mushallal near Qudaid by the Red Sea, Al-Lat at Ta'if, and Al-'Uzza in the valley of Nakhlah. It is reported that 'Amr also dug up the idols of Noah's people, Wadd, Suwa', Yaghuth, Ya'uq and Nasr, and gave them to the tribes. When Makkah was conquered, 360 idols stood around the Ka'bah. The Prophet ﷺ broke them all.
list:
- Hubal: inside the Ka'bah
- Al-Lat: at Ta'if
- Al-'Uzza: in the valley of Nakhlah
- Manat: at Al-Mushallal, near Qudaid

## arabia-worship
title: The Ways of Idol Worship
word: Azlam
mood: ember
when: Before Islam
place: Arabia
look: text
tags: faith
They gave themselves to their idols. They swore by them, called on them in hardship, and circled and bowed before them. They sacrificed to them [5:3] and set aside shares of their crops and herds for them [6:136]. Some camels and sheep were dedicated to the idols and left free and unused. The Qur'an denies that Allah ever made such a law [5:103]. They said they worshipped idols only to be brought nearer to Allah [39:3].

They decided their affairs by drawing arrows marked yes and no. They trusted soothsayers, diviners and astrologers. They believed it rained because of a star and read omens in the flight of birds.

Some of Ibrahim's ways remained: honouring the Ka'bah, pilgrimage, the stand at 'Arafat and sacrifice. But they were bent out of shape. Quraish, calling themselves Al-Hums, would not go out to 'Arafat with everyone else [2:199]. Pilgrims from outside had to circle the House in clothes from Al-Hums, or else naked [7:31]. In the sacred state, people entered their houses through holes in the back wall [2:189].

## arabia-faiths
title: Jews, Christians and Others
word: Najran
mood: dusk
img: solidus
when: 587 BCE – 6th century CE
y: 520
place: Arabia
look: text
tags: faith
Jews came into Arabia in two waves. The first came after Nebuchadnezzar destroyed their temple in 587 BCE, and the second after Rome took Palestine in 70 CE. They settled in Yathrib, Khaibar and Taima', built forts, and drew some Arab tribes to their faith. When Islam came, Banu Qainuqa', An-Nadir and Quraizah were among them.

A king of Yemen took Judaism back home from Yathrib. His son, Dhu Nawas, was the one who burned the Christians of Najran in October 523 CE. Christianity came into Arabia with the Abyssinians and the Romans. A preacher named Fimion won Najran to it. Ghassan, Taghlib and Tai' became Christian tribes. Near Persia some Arabs followed the Magian faith, and a few still kept the old Sabian religion.

All of these had little hold on the lives of the Arabs. The idol worshippers claimed Ibrahim's religion but were far from it. Among the Jews and Christians of the time, their religion had become much changed from what it once was.

## arabia-society
title: Society in the Days of Ignorance
word: Tribe
mood: parchment
img: inscription
when: Before Islam
place: Arabia
look: text
tags: society
Among the noble families, women were honoured, had a say in their affairs, and blood would be shed to defend them. Yet the family was ruled by its men, and a woman's guardian decided her marriage. Of the kinds of union people made, Islam kept only marriage as we know it. There was no limit on the number of wives, and divorce was in the husband's hands [2:229].

Some held their children dear, while others buried their baby daughters alive in fear of poverty or shame [16:58-59] [81:8]. The Qur'an forbade killing children for fear of want [17:31].

Loyalty to the tribe ruled everything. "Support your brother, wrongdoer or wronged" was taken at its word. Pride and rivalry kept cousin tribes at war for years: Aws and Khazraj, 'Abs and Dhubyan, Bakr and Taghlib. Only the four sacred months brought a pause, when people could trade and live in peace.

## arabia-economy
title: Trade and Want
word: 'Ukaz
mood: sand
img: boswellia
when: Before Islam
place: Arabia
look: text
tags: society
Trade was the main way to make a living, but caravans needed safe roads and peace between the tribes, and these were rare outside the sacred months. In those months the great markets met: 'Ukaz, Dhul-Majaz and Majannah.

Arabia had almost no industry. Weaving and tanning came from Yemen, Heerah and the edges of Syria. There was a little farming and herding, and the women spun wool, though war threatened even that. For most people, poverty, hunger and want of food and clothing were the shape of life.

## arabia-virtues
title: What Was Noble in Them
word: Karam
mood: parchment
when: Before Islam
place: Arabia
look: list
tags: society, character
For all their vices, the Arabs held virtues the world admired. These were the qualities, together with the land's place in the world, that fitted them to carry the Message. Islam would keep them and refine them.

A man with nothing but the camel his family lived on would slaughter it for a guest. Nearly half their poetry is about hospitality. To make a promise was to owe a debt: they would lose children and home before breaking their word.
list:
- Hospitality: competing to feed the guest, and paying blood money to stop a feud
- Keeping their word: a promise was a debt, kept at any cost
- Honour: no humiliation was borne, no injustice accepted
- Resolve: no chance of honour was let pass, even at the cost of life
- Forbearance: valued all the more by a people quick to anger
- Simplicity: desert life kept them truthful and far from intrigue
